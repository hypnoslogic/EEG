Read the brain, not just the number

A referenced, open tutorial for anaesthesia registrars, medical officers and consultants on the raw EEG, the spectrogram and the processed indices on GE Entropy, BIS and SedLine monitors.

Why this exists

The brain is the target organ of every general anaesthetic, yet it is the organ we monitor least well. Most anaesthetists who use EEG monitors read only the index number. That number comes from a proprietary algorithm, lags several seconds behind the brain, is distorted by muscle activity, ketamine, nitrous oxide and age, and may miss burst suppression altogether{{cite:delphi2026,sbi2025}}. Every monitor already shows the raw trace and a spectrogram. Learning to read them takes training rather than new equipment{{cite:sbi2025}}.

The approach here follows Purdon and colleagues: each anaesthetic drug acts on particular receptors in particular circuits, and so leaves its own signature in the EEG. You can learn to recognise these signatures and use them to titrate anaesthesia{{cite:purdon2015}}. Clinical applications come from Lee, Egan and Johnson{{cite:lee2021}}, updated with trials and reviews published since 2021.

How the course is organised

The learning outcomes come from the 2026 international Delphi consensus on EEG education in anaesthesiology{{cite:delphi2026}}. Each page lists the outcomes it covers, and the curriculum map shows all of them. The order of the pages follows the Safe Brain Initiative EEG boot camp{{cite:sbi2025}}: why monitor, what the monitor shows, how drugs, artefacts and nociception change it, how patients differ, and then putting it into practice.

Content is tagged at two levels. {{basic}} is what every anaesthetist using an EEG monitor should know. {{adv}} is aimed at people who teach EEG, do research or want to go further.

  1. Where the EEG comes from and how it is analysed

    Neurophysiology, frequency bands, decibels, the spectrum, the spectrogram, median frequency and SEF95.

  2. Reading the raw EEG and recognising artefacts

    Awake to isoelectric, the phases of an anaesthetic, burst suppression, and the ShARP approach to artefacts.

  3. Drug signatures

    Propofol, the volatile agents, ketamine, dexmedetomidine and nitrous oxide, and why one index cannot describe them all.

  4. Processed EEG: Entropy, BIS and SedLine

    What each monitor computes, what the numbers mean, and where they mislead.

  5. Using EEG in theatre

    Step-by-step approaches for induction, maintenance and emergence, plus nociception, the vulnerable brain, TIVA, haemodynamics and substance use.

  6. The evidence

    A living summary of the trials and meta-analyses on delirium, mortality and awareness.

  7. Under the hood

    Window length and frequency resolution, how the figures were generated, and the companion real-time analysis script.

About the images

Every EEG trace, spectrum and spectrogram on this site is synthetic. Each was generated to have the spectral shape described in the cited literature, so learners see clean examples, no patient data is involved, and every figure can be shared freely. Real clinical examples are linked from Further learning.

This is a living resource. Each page shows the date it was last reviewed, and changes are logged on the roadmap and changelog.
