Processed EEG: Entropy, BIS and SedLine

Module 4 · about 20 minutes · Basic with an Advanced section

By the end of this page you should be able to

  • Describe the concepts behind processed EEG, focusing on reading the spectrum rather than on how an index is built. Delphi PR4
  • Explain SEF, EMG, burst suppression ratio and signal quality index. Delphi PR6
  • Compare the strengths and limitations of processed and raw EEG. Delphi PR5
  • Critique EEG indices and recognise when they are unreliable. Delphi PR14
  • Use processed EEG to guide anaesthesia alongside the raw trace and DSA. Delphi PR2
The message of this page

Don't trust the number alone. An index is an alarm that prompts you to look, not the answer. Every monitor shows the raw EEG and a DSA as well, and using them avoids or reduces most of the index's known failings[4]. When five commercial monitors were given identical EEG signals they gave discordant recommendations (below)[11]. If you can read the raw EEG, you can check any of them.

What processing does

All the major monitors follow roughly the same steps:

  1. Acquire frontal EEG through a disposable sensor and check impedance.
  2. Clean it by detecting and rejecting artefact, and grade the signal quality.
  3. Extract features: band powers, spectral edge, measures of order or entropy, and detection of suppression.
  4. Combine these with a proprietary algorithm into a dimensionless 0–100 scale, where lower means deeper.
  5. Smooth the result over time. Each index reflects roughly the last 15–30 s of EEG, plus processing delay, so it lags behind the brain[2,41].

The indices were developed by recording EEG and behavioural responses in cohorts of adult patients, often given GABAergic drugs, and fitting algorithms to them. Some used regression, one used classification of visually scored EEG, and one used the entropy of the signal[1]. Most algorithms are proprietary.

The extra numbers on the screen

SQI
Signal quality index. If it is low, ignore the index until you have fixed the signal.
EMG
Frontal muscle activity, which raises indices. It goes away under neuromuscular block, which can make an index fall with no change in hypnosis[4]. On Entropy, read the RE–SE gap.
SR / BSR
Suppression ratio: the percentage of a recent window in which the EEG was suppressed. Any non-zero value means look at the raw trace. Automated detection can still miss suppression[4].
SEF95
The frequency below which 95% of power lies (Module 1). It is useful as a trend but depends on the drug, and it becomes erratic during burst suppression.
DSA
The density spectral array. Read it at least as often as the number.

Five monitors, one EEG, different answers

Hight and colleagues took 52 recorded EEG segments showing patterns of lightening anaesthesia, like those seen during emergence, and played the identical signals into five commercial monitors: BIS, Entropy, qCON, SedLine and Narcotrend. The monitors gave discordant clinical recommendations in response to the same signals, so a clinician following one device could reasonably act differently from a clinician following another[11].

The lesson for this course: each index is one manufacturer's interpretation of the EEG. If you look at the EEG yourself, you are no longer dependent on any single algorithm.

A routine for every glance at the monitor

This sequence follows the boot camp's algorithmic approach[4]:

  1. Quality. Is the SQI good? Are the electrodes on? Is diathermy running?
  2. Raw trace. What pattern is it: awake, beta, slow-delta plus alpha, theta-filled, burst suppression? Is there EMG or another artefact?
  3. DSA. Where is the power, and how has it changed over the last few minutes? Is there an alpha band? Stripes? Rising beta?
  4. Numbers. Do the index, SR and SEF agree with what you have just seen? If not, believe the EEG and look for the reason (drug, EMG, age, artefact).
  5. Clinical context. Drugs and doses, end-tidal or TCI concentration, haemodynamics, surgical stimulus. Then act (Module 5).

Advanced Limits of quantitative EEG metrics

Every quantitative feature has assumptions built in (Delphi QT1):

  • Band definitions: relative band power depends on where the edges are and what the "total" range is (compare the two schemes).
  • Window length: longer windows resolve frequency better but follow changes more slowly (Module 7).
  • Reference range: SEF and median frequency change if you calculate them over 0.1–30 Hz instead of 0.5–45 Hz.
  • Non-stationarity: burst suppression and spindles break the assumption that the signal is stable within a window.
  • Population calibration: an algorithm fitted to adults given GABAergic drugs carries its biases into children, older adults and other drugs[1].
