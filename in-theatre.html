Using EEG in theatre

Module 5 · about 30 minutes · Basic

By the end of this page you should be able to

  • Integrate EEG monitoring into the whole anaesthetic, from baseline to emergence. Delphi AM1 Delphi FD8
  • Use the EEG to avoid both inadequate and excessive anaesthesia. Delphi AM2
  • Recognise nociception-related EEG patterns and interpret them alongside clinical signs. Delphi NC11 Delphi NC12
  • Adjust management for older, younger and individually variable patients, and identify those at risk of neurocognitive problems. Delphi PV1 Delphi PV2 Delphi PV3 Delphi PV6 Delphi PV10 Delphi PV11
  • Communicate EEG findings clearly to the team. Delphi AM3

The boot camp turns its teaching into simple, problem-based algorithms that clinicians can use straight away[4]. This page follows the same approach, and draws on Lee et al. for the clinical scenarios[2].

Which patients?

Lee et al. describe four settings where pEEG monitoring is likely to be most useful[2]:

  • TIVA with neuromuscular block, where there is no end-tidal concentration to confirm drug delivery and no movement to warn you.
  • The vulnerable brain: older or frail patients, cerebrovascular disease, intracranial pathology, severe systemic illness.
  • Haemodynamic instability, to help decide between changing the anaesthetic depth and giving vasoactive drugs or fluid.
  • Known or suspected substance use, where anaesthetic requirements are hard to predict.

An expert consensus statement recommended end-tidal gas monitoring with alarms, or pEEG, to reduce the risk of awareness with recall in patients receiving general anaesthesia[19]. The guideline landscape is summarised on the evidence page.

Phase by phase

Before induction

  1. Apply the sensor, check impedance and SQI, and confirm the raw trace looks clean (Module 2).
  2. Record the awake baseline. Note the amplitude. Is this an older or frail brain with little alpha to start with?
  3. Decide which pattern you will aim for given the drugs you plan to use (Module 3).

Induction

  1. Watch the raw trace, not the index. The index lags. Slow-delta waves appearing within 10–30 s of a propofol bolus fits with loss of consciousness[1].
  2. If burst suppression appears after a standard dose, especially in an older patient, this patient is sensitive. Note it and reduce what follows[1,28].
  3. Expect a brief arousal pattern at laryngoscopy and intubation (see nociception, below).

Maintenance: too light, about right or too deep?

The boot camp frames the core decision as three questions: is the patient too light, about right, or too deep[4]? For propofol and the volatile agents:

Possibly too light

Alpha fading. Beta (and gamma) rising. SEF95 and index climbing. The raw trace getting smaller and faster.

First rule out EMG, nociception, ketamine or N₂O, and artefact. Then check delivery: pump, line, cannula, vaporiser.

About right

Robust slow-delta plus a clear frontal alpha band. Theta filling in if on 1 MAC or more of volatile. No suppression. Index in the device's usual range.

Hold steady. Reassess at every change in stimulus or drug.

Possibly too deep

Any suppression: flat stretches, stripes on the DSA, SR above 0. Alpha power falling. Very low index or SEF.

Reduce the hypnotic if haemodynamics and surgery allow. Look for other causes: hypotension, hypothermia, ischaemia.

Nociception or too light?

"Depth" is not one dimension. Hypnosis versus arousal and nociception versus antinociception are separate, though connected, axes. The cortical EEG mostly reports the hypnotic axis. Nociception is processed mainly in the spinal cord and subcortical structures, but as an arousing stimulus it can show up indirectly in the EEG[4]:

  • Alpha drop-out: the frontal alpha band weakens or disappears.
  • Beta arousal: beta activity increases, and SEF and the index rise.
  • "Paradoxical" delta arousal: slow-delta power increases after a stimulus. It can be mistaken for deepening.
  1. Is there a stimulus? Incision, traction, laryngoscopy, pinning, a tourniquet?
  2. Look at the rest of the picture: heart rate, blood pressure, movement, tearing, sweating, pupils. EEG changes must be interpreted together with clinical signs[3].
  3. If the pattern fits nociception, treat nociception first. Give analgesia: an opioid bolus, a block or local anaesthetic. If you only deepen the hypnotic, you risk going too deep once the stimulus stops.
  4. If there is no stimulus and alpha has gone, check drug delivery before anything else. In one published case, a propofol infusion that had been interrupted during spine surgery first showed itself as loss of the alpha band on the spectrogram, before the index changed[45].

Emergence

  1. After turning off the anaesthetic, expect the zipper on the DSA: slow-delta and alpha fade, and beta and gamma return[1].
  2. If you switched to high-flow N₂O, do not be alarmed by a transient large slow-delta wave in the first few minutes[1].
  3. Watch the path through emergence. Patterns that skip the usual alpha-rich stage were associated with delirium in the recovery room, and this was more pronounced with ketamine or N₂O[29]. At present this is a reason for closer observation, not a validated intervention.

The vulnerable brain

"Vulnerable brain" describes patients who may be unusually sensitive to anaesthetics or at risk of postoperative neurocognitive problems: advanced age, neurovascular disease, intracranial pathology, traumatic brain injury, or severe infection or metabolic disturbance[2].

  • EEG power falls in every band with age, alpha most of all, and burst suppression is more common in older patients[26].
  • In one analysis, each 1 dB fall in frontal alpha power increased the odds of burst suppression 1.33-fold. The authors proposed low frontal alpha as an EEG phenotype of the vulnerable brain[25].
  • Lower intraoperative frontal alpha power correlated with poorer preoperative cognitive function in a small study of older adults[30].
  • More intraoperative burst suppression has been associated with more postoperative delirium. Suppression occurring at low volatile concentrations was a particularly strong predictor, suggesting it marks a sensitive brain[27,28].
Association, not proof

These are associations. Whether preventing suppression reduces delirium is still uncertain. The trials disagree (Module 6). Sensible practice is to avoid excessive anaesthesia, treat physiological and metabolic problems promptly, and use the EEG as one part of a wider approach to perioperative brain health[2].

Practical points for older patients: start with lower doses. Look for suppression early and often in the raw trace, because automated detection may miss low-voltage suppression[4]. Remember the index is not age-adjusted[4], and a small alpha band may be normal for this patient. Treat early suppression at modest doses as a sign of sensitivity.

Children: the EEG develops with age, and adult-derived indices are less reliable in children[1]. Even so, a 2026 meta-analysis of nine RCTs found less emergence delirium with EEG-guided anaesthesia in children (Module 6)[10]. A paediatric section is planned.

Every patient varies, not only the old and the young. Use each patient's own baseline and response as the reference, rather than a population number (Delphi PV6).

TIVA, neuromuscular block and awareness

With a volatile agent, the end-tidal concentration confirms the drug is reaching the patient. With TIVA there is no such check. Disconnections, tissued cannulas, empty syringes and pump errors can go unnoticed, and neuromuscular block removes movement as a warning sign[2].

  • In the UK's NAP5 audit, which relied on patients reporting awareness spontaneously, the overall incidence was about 1 in 19,000. It was 1 in 14,000 with TIVA, and about 1 in 8,300 with TIVA plus neuromuscular block. NAP5 judged that many TIVA cases were avoidable and related to drug delivery, and suggested pEEG for TIVA with block[20,2].
  • A prospective study that interviewed every patient found a much higher rate, about 1%. The difference shows how much depends on how awareness is detected[21].
  • Propofol requirements vary widely between patients. Target-controlled infusion targets of roughly 3–6 µg/mL are commonly quoted, adjusted for opioid co-administration, anxiety and frailty[24,2].
TIVA with neuromuscular block: a checklist

Keep the cannula visible, check the pump and line regularly, and watch the raw EEG and DSA continuously, not only the index. A loss of alpha with a rise in beta, with no surgical stimulus to explain it, means check the propofol first[45].

Haemodynamics: change the anaesthetic or support the circulation?

When blood pressure moves, the right response may be to change anaesthetic depth, to give a vasoactive drug or fluid, or both. Lee et al. propose using the pEEG to help decide[2]. The table below is adapted from their decision matrix. It is based on expert opinion with limited supporting evidence.

  • The "triple low" (mean arterial pressure under 80 mmHg, BIS under 40 and MAC under 0.8) was associated with longer stay and higher mortality in a large retrospective analysis[31]. In a later real-time randomised trial, alerting clinicians to triple lows did not reduce mortality. Clinicians ignored about half of the alerts, so the trial could not properly test the idea[32,2].
  • Haemorrhage: shock shrinks the volume of distribution and slows clearance, so normal doses of intravenous anaesthetic give higher concentrations and deeper effect[34]. In one case report, BIS fell before the blood pressure crisis during major haemorrhage under propofol TIVA[33]. A falling index or new suppression during bleeding is a reason to reduce the anaesthetic.

Patients who use substances

Requirements can be higher or lower than expected, and the EEG gives you a patient-specific guide[2]:

  • Chronic opioid use: perhaps unexpectedly, chronic opioid users needed less end-tidal sevoflurane to keep BIS below 50 (0.84% vs 1.18%) in the absence of surgical stimulation[35]. Chronic pain may also alter theta-band activity, with uncertain clinical meaning[2].
  • Buprenorphine: current advice is generally to continue it through the perioperative period. Expect altered interactions with full agonists and titrate to effect, using pEEG and end-tidal monitoring[2].
  • Alcohol: acute intoxication slows the EEG and BIS fell as blood alcohol rose[36]. Chronic heavy drinkers needed more propofol for induction (about 2.7 vs 2.2 mg/kg) and for maintenance[37].

Communicating EEG findings

Describe what you see, what you think it means and what you will do, in that order (Delphi AM3). For example:

"The frontal alpha has gone and there's new beta since they started the retraction. The index has gone from 42 to 58 over two minutes. Heart rate is up as well. I think this is nociception rather than light anaesthesia, so I'm giving remifentanil and holding the propofol."

At handover, include the EEG pattern, any burst suppression or sensitivity seen at induction, and the drug or doses that produced a stable pattern.
